\section{Introduction|The Network}

    The network we built is a representation of the ingredients used in the preparation of popular dishes in major Indian cuisines. Our base network is bipartite; with the cuisines \emph{(Kashmiri, Mughlai, Gujarati)} and ingredients as nodes. An edge is always between a cuisine and an ingredient, and the edge weights signify the frequency of an ingredient in a given cuisine. We then project the graph onto cuisines and ingredients to obtain the respective one-mode projections. Nodes in a 'cuisines projection' represent the distinct Indian cuisines in our dataset and an edge between them indicates a shared ingredient, weighted by the number of shared ingredients, and vice-versa for the ingredient projection. This representation was unanimously agreed by the team as the most elegant form. The only alternative that crept up into the discussions was a possibility to replace the individual ingredients with a collective ingredient-type, such as \emph{'spices', 'fruits', 'grains',} etc. Such an idea was however, immediately dismissed to avoid ambiguity and bias in the ingredient groupings and in an attempt to preserve information in the base network.



\section{Data Collection}
    Our \href{https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/sooryaprakash12/cleaned-indian-recipes-dataset?resource=download}{\textit{primary dataset}} comprises of around 6000 recipes obtained from an open-source website called \href{https://www.kaggle.com/}{\textit{kaggle}}. The dataset itself is a cleaned version that simplifies data processing from the original | \href{https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/sukhmandeepsinghbrar/indian-food-dataset/data}{\textit{Indian Food Dataset}}. The dataset was downloaded as a csv file after the requirement of free kaggle sign-in. Although slightly old, we obtained data on the cooking instructions, ingredients, estimated cooking time, cuisine it belonged to and the reference URL for each recipe.



\section{Data Processing}
    The downloaded dataset required multiple processing, some of which were forced later on due to errors in the initial attempts. Our goal was to convert the dataset into a standard edge-list listing each edge between a recipe and its ingredients.\\
    The sheer volume of the dataset proved to be the biggest challenge in this process, followed by ingredient redundancies, which were a consequence of: linguistic differences, different forms of the same ingredient, whitespace differences, spelling mistakes and a few cleaning flaws (such as measurements attached to the ingredients: \emph{sugar} vs \emph{1tbsp sugar}). The redundancies in the dataset resulted in duplicate nodes which, in addition to further cluttering the graph, split an ingredient's importance significantly.

    \subsection{Setting the standards}
        Standardizing the ingredient names was one of the biggest decisions we had to make, one that also caused a lot of discussion. The specific form of an ingredient makes a key difference in a dish's preparation, yet it splits its importance and misleads the analysis, such as wheat vs wheat flour, table salt vs rock salt and sugar vs sugar syrup. Certain differences however, such as leaves and roots of the same plant, dramatically influence the dish. \textit{"Where do we draw the line?"} was the most important question we faced, one that was foundational to any further analysis. In the end, the team reached consensus on the following:
        \begin{itemize}
          \item We reverted most powders and pastes back to their native ingredients as they can be seen as a processing step and not a different source ingredient.
          \item Different parts of a plant and different varieties of it were considered separately as that is not a difference that could be compensated in the kitchen. 
          \item We chose to keep factory processed ingredients, such as masala mixes, spring roll sheets, gulab jamun since they too are not usually made in the kitchen (as an ingredient for another dish), and since further factorisations of such ingredients demanded a more intense level of involvement.
        \end{itemize}
        These decisions were agreed upon as to make the most sense for the questions we intended to ask to the network. Different approaches to the problem may require different normalizing standards.
    
    \subsection{The Filtering and Cleaning Process}
        The first step towards processing the code was converting the raw data in the form of procedures to a csv file of ingredients and recipes. The python script 'cvtr.py' parses the same to form the nodes and edges and it also takes care of most of the common spelling variations and quantitative information \textit{(like litres and teaspoons)}. Two files were made, one nodes file - containing all node properties, and an edges file which contains all the links.\\
        
        The nodes and edge files were then used for network analysis. However, during the analysis, some data discrepancies were very evident, which forced us to revisit the files and a long, tedious manual cleaning followed. The manual process was conducted on the edges file with aid from \textit{LibreOffice Calc}'s 'Find and Replace' tool, with the changes being carried over into the nodes file using the 'nodes cleaner' python script. This was an iterative process that required frequent revisits on the discovery of new errors, the final nodes file (nodes 9.0) is about the nineth iteration of cleaning. We still suspect a few errors in the final data which may be negligible.

    \subsection{Projections, Coordinates and colors}
        The completely processed nodes and edge files were used to obtain the one-mode projections onto the recipes, cuisines and ingredients. The nodes file was also modified to include separate columns with colors and coordinates specified as node properties corresponding to the respective cuisines. The coordinates were obtained as a CSV file using the help of AI. This was to better represent the network visually on Gephi Software. All of these modifications were performed using python scripts.

\section{Network Topology}
    The network is built as a sparse bipartite graph with $n = 3{,}933$ recipes and $568$ unique ingredients connected by $m = 46{,}669$ edges, giving a global bipartite density of $0.0209$ (Figure~\ref{fig:topo_overview}). On average, each recipe uses $12$ ingredients (rounded off to the nearest integer), while each ingredient appears in $82$ recipes. Projecting the graph into one-mode networks gives a denser ingredient network (density $0.1157$; $18{,}635$ edges) than recipe network (density $0.0441$; $340{,}974$ edges). Both projections have heavy-tailed degree distributions: ingredient degrees range from $3$ to $509$ (median $33$, mean $65.6$), and recipe degrees range from $0$ to $922$ (median $158$, mean $173.4$). Even after removing $49$ common hub ingredients, the network stays strongly connected---$100\%$ of ingredients ($568$ nodes, $1$ component) and $91.5\%$ of recipes ($3{,}597$ nodes) in one giant component (Figure~\ref{fig:topo_overview}).\\
    Bipartite density by region shows many ingredients shared within regions than across the full graph ($0.0212$; Figure~\ref{fig:regional_density}). East India has the highest density ($0.0582$), followed by West India ($0.0421$), North India ($0.0411$), and South India ($0.0361$).

\section{Model Comparisons}
    To analyze the structural characteristics of the observed network, we compared it with five standard bipartite network models while keeping the two node sets and the total number of edges constant. The models, their construction strategies, and the specific structural properties they are designed to examine are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:models}. 
    
    \begin{table*}[!t]
        \centering
        \small
        \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
        \begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{|p{2.8cm}|X|X|}
         \hline
            \textbf{Model} & \textbf{Construction} & \textbf{Purpose} \\
         \hline
            Erdős--Rényi (ER)
            & Selects 46,669 distinct possible recipe--ingredient pairs uniformly at random.
            & A simple random wiring baseline. It removes the observed arrangement of links. \\
            \hline
            Watts--Strogatz-like (WS)
            & Starts from a ring topology and rewires edges with $\beta = 0.1$.
            & Introduces local structure and rewiring to test whether local organization can explain clustering. \\
            \hline
            Barabási--Albert-like (BA)
            & Preferential attachment-style model with $\alpha = 1.0$ and no closure step.
            & Tests degree heterogeneity arising from preferential attachment. \\
            \hline
            Holme--Kim-like (HK)
            & Growth-style model with $p_{\text{close}} = 0.3$ and $\alpha = 1.0$.
            & Adds a closure mechanism to the growth model. \\
            \hline

    \subsection{Degree distribution}
        The degree distributions describe how connections are distributed among recipe and ingredient nodes. Although all models have the same number of nodes and edges, their degree distributions differ considerably.\\
        
        \textbf{Recipe nodes}: The original network shows a degree distribution concentrated around moderate degrees, with a tail extending towards higher degrees. The WS and DP models reproduce parts of this distribution more closely than ER, BA and HK. The BA and HK models show broader tails, indicating greater degree heterogeneity while ER produces a more random distribution.\\
        
        \textbf{Ingredient nodes}: The original network has a highly heterogeneous degree distribution. A small number of ingredients connect to many recipes, while numerous ingredients appear in relatively few recipes. The generated models do not reproduce this distribution equally well.

    \subsection{Overall comparison}
        The comparative results show that no single alternative model reproduces every structural property of the original network.
        
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{ER:} Provides a random baseline but substantially underestimates clustering and four-cycle structure.
        
            \item \textbf{WS:} Reproduces high clustering but does not reproduce all observed degree and shared-neighbour patterns.
        
            \item \textbf{BA:} Introduces degree heterogeneity through preferential attachment, but its generated networks are fragmented and have lower clustering and four-cycle counts than the original.
        
            \item \textbf{HK:} Adds a closure mechanism, but it also produces fragmented networks and does not recover the original network's four-cycle structure.
        
            \item \textbf{DP:} Reproduces the original four-cycle count and mean shared-neighbour counts more closely than the other generated models. It also maintains a single connected component and relatively short paths.
        \end{itemize}

\section{Correlations and Robustness}

    \subsection{Degree Correlations}
    Correlations measure the tendency of like nodes to share an edge. Two common measures used for the identifying degree correlations are the pearson coefficient ($r$) and average nearest neighbour degree (ANND). A positive $r$-value suggests assortativity \textit{(like pairs with like)}, negative suggests dis-assortativity and zero suggests neutrality. A similar criteria appears for the slope of 'ANND vs degree' plots. Both measures were computed as detailed in \texttt{correlations.ipynb} using a python script.
    
    \begin{figure}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{knnplots.png}
        \caption{Correlation analysis on weighted ingredient and recipe projections}
        \label{fig:correlation}
    \end{figure}
        
    From Figure ~\ref{fig:correlation}, the plot for recipes projection behaves erradic and disagrees with the $r$ value, probably due to incorrect assumptions in the formulations of the measures. Ingredient projection appears to behave more predictably if we attribute the falling curve to finite-size effects which are commonly observed in real networks. In layman terms, we were unable to find any evidence on ingredient pairings or mismatches from degree correlations. It is possible that certain pairings were cancelled out by other mismatches when taking the average, but our study cannot prove them.

\section{Ingredient Co-occurrence and Food Pairing}
\label{sec:cooccurrence}
    Adapting the food-pairing hypothesis~\cite{ahn2011} from flavour chemistry to recipe usage, we test whether ingredient pairs co-occur beyond popularity expectations and whether those associations persist across cuisines.

    \subsection{Method}
        From \texttt{nodes\_9.0} and \texttt{edges\_6.0}, we evaluate $8{,}911$ pairs among $134$ ingredients appearing in $\ge 30$ recipes (excluding salt and water). Degree-preserving Curveball trades~\cite{strona2014} generate $500$ null matrices under a \emph{pooled} model (trades across all recipes) and a \emph{stratified} model (trades restricted within $21$ cuisine strata, pooling $19$ cuisines with $<30$ recipes into \emph{other} and treating \emph{Indian} as unknown).

        Standardized scores $z = (\text{observed} - \text{null mean})/\text{null s.d.}$ (normal two-sided $p$ for null mean $\ge 5$, Poisson tail otherwise) are Benjamini--Hochberg corrected~\cite{benjamini1995} at $q<0.05$ (\texttt{cooccurrence\_test.py}, \texttt{plot\_cooccurrence.py}; output in \texttt{cooccurrence\_results.csv}). Comparing significance across both nulls classifies pairs as \emph{universal} (both nulls, same sign), \emph{regional} (pooled only), \emph{masked} (stratified only), \emph{sign flip} (opposite signs), or \emph{ns}.

        Most significant pairings are \emph{universal} ($1{,}664$ pairs; Table~\ref{tab:cooc_classes}), persisting even when cuisine is controlled (Figure~\ref{fig:cooc_panel}a). In contrast, $563$ \emph{regional} pairings are driven by shared cuisine geography, while $145$ \emph{masked} pairings only emerge within individual cuisines once cross-cuisine variance is removed. A single pair-mustard oil and sugar-flips sign, appearing favoured overall yet avoided within cuisines.

        Extreme positive pairs capture canonical flavour and prep pairings (Figure~\ref{fig:cooc_panel}b), led by green with sweet chutney ($27$ vs.\ $0.4$ expected, $z=44.0$) and curry leaves with mustard seeds ($827$ vs.\ $344$, $z=37.9$). Extreme avoidances separate competing fats or incompatible staples, such as ghee with sunflower oil ($189$ vs.\ $410$, $z=-16.3$) and milk with red chilli ($39$ vs.\ $175$, $z=-16.3$). Thresholding at $z\ge 12$ under both nulls isolates $71$ cuisine-invariant links across $45$ ingredients, forming eight modular culinary clusters (Figure~\ref{fig:cooc_panel}c): curry aromatics (onion, tomato, ginger, garlic), sweets (milk, sugar, nuts), biryani whole spices, vegetables, chaat accompaniments (chutneys, sev), southern tempering (curry leaves, mustard seeds, asafoetida), green coriander--oil, and bajra--millet flour. These clusters confirm that co-occurrence structure reflects functional culinary preference rather than raw ingredient frequency.

\section{Ingredient diversity}
    To understand why regional sub-networks are much denser than the whole graph, we tested how two key ingredient groups---whole and ground spices, and culinary leaves---are distributed across Indian cuisines. Refer figure ~\ref{fig:regional_signatures} for the numerical backing, which have been omitted in text to save space.
    
    \textbf{Spices across regions (Figure~\ref{fig:spices}):} Red chilli and turmeric act as a shared base across almost all cuisines. North Indian and Mughlai cuisines rely mostly on warm spices such as cumin, cardamom and cinnamon. In contrast, South Indian and coastal cuisines use mustard seeds and tamarind more.
    
    \textbf{Culinary leaves (Figure~\ref{fig:leaves}):} Leafy ingredients create the sharpest North--South boundary in the network. Curry leaves dominate South Indian and coastal recipes but are rare in Northern and Eastern cuisines, while bay leaves are found mostly in Northern, Eastern, and Hyderabadi cooking. Secondary herbs such as mint and methi leaves further separate local clusters.

\section{Limitations and Possibilities}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Classification of ingredients: Most dishes can be made without supplementary ingredients, whereas the absence of key ingredients will 'break' the dish. Such a hierarchy would transform the topology and yield interesting robustness analysis. We dropped the idea since such a classification is data starved and would be biased and heavily time consuming.
        \item Origins of some dishes are controversial or not well-known, yet we followed the original dataset for their classification. Ambiguous dishes are classified under a broader region like \textit{'Indian'} or \textit{'South Indian'}, which are sources of information leakage.
        \item Regional representations vary within the dataset. North-eastern cuisine is heavily under-represented in our study whereas Maharashtrian and Karnatak dishes are well represented.
        \item The approximate preparation times available from our dataset could have been better utilized, perhaps to study the varying efforts and time required for preparing different Indian cuisines.
        \item We initially thought of drawing a correlation between famines and the robustness to failure, but the data we had did not correlate as much and was mostly random. 
        
    \end{itemize}
